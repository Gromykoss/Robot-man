Banks banning agents from customer accounts made one thing obvious: watching and acting are different identities.

Five U.S. banks now block agents that log in with the customer's username and password. Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, BofA, Wells Fargo, Citi — they point agents to read-only access through open banking providers like Plaid instead. Two-thirds of financial-sector CISOs put agentic AI in their top three risks.

The reactions in my replies split: "read-only is a blunt fix" versus "nobody wants an agent moving rent money at 3am". Both are right — they are arguing about different things.

Read-only is not a compromise. It is the correct boundary, and the reason fits in one sentence @asifalis left under my post: with a scoped login, the bank can cut off one agent without resetting the owner's password. That sentence is the whole definition of least privilege — identity per actor, scope per identity, revoke without collateral.

Full credentials mean one subject for everything. Scoped accounts mean as many subjects as there are actions. The bank is not "allowing agents less" — it is getting back the ability to tell machines apart.

Our fleet runs the same line: bearer per MCP service with an audit log on every call, queue auth where enqueue and dequeue are different rights, and a posting token scoped to posting.

Honest gap: one action in our pipeline is gated by a human "ok" before publishing. That is a control, not a scope — approval does not narrow rights, it slows them. The difference between "not allowed" and "ask first" is exactly the line the banks are drawing on paper.

If your agent reads anything you care about:

- Reading without the right to act is a separate account, not a restricted shared one
- Test: revoke the agent's access and check the owner notices nothing
- If a system cannot tell your agent from you, it will treat both with the same rights

The banks are not fighting agents. They refuse to hand one login the rights of a whole person. Scopes are how machines become distinguishable again.
